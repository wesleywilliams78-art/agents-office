Working capital cycle management
Introduction
This chapter defines how Imran’s Diner manages its working capital cycle — the time and cash tied up between paying suppliers and receiving payment from customers. In a QSR operation where most sales are cash or immediate card settlement, the working capital cycle is typically short and favourable: customers pay instantly, but suppliers grant credit terms. The risk is not the cycle itself — it is losing control of it: paying suppliers early, holding too much stock, or failing to enforce agreed credit terms. This chapter provides the framework for keeping cash circulating efficiently.
Minimum Operating Standards
The working capital cycle for each unit must be understood and documented: (a) Inventory holding period — average days from receiving stock to selling it. Perishables: 3–5 days. Dry goods and packaging: 14–30 days. These are consequences of the par levels set in Chapter 5.3, not an independent control: if par levels are calculated correctly, holding period follows. A perishable holding period drifting above 5 days means par levels are set too high for current volumes and should be recalculated, not that stock should be run down manually. (b) Receivables period — for a QSR takeaway with predominantly cash/card/delivery-platform sales, this is short but not always zero (same-day to T+2 settlement from card processors is typical; delivery platforms such as Deliveroo/Just Eat/Uber Eats typically pay out weekly, which is a materially longer receivables period worth tracking separately). [Tawheed to confirm: card processor settlement terms, and the payout schedule and commission rate for each delivery platform] This is the largest receivables exposure in the business and it is routinely overlooked. A delivery platform paying weekly in arrears means the unit is carrying up to a week of delivery revenue as a receivable at any moment, against a counterparty the franchisee has no leverage over. It must be shown separately in the 13-week cash flow forecast rather than netted into revenue. (c) Payables period — average days from receiving a supplier invoice to paying it (target: use the full agreed credit terms, typically 30 days net where credit is available). Note the current position: the business buys predominantly from cash and carry, which is pay-on-collection, so the payables period is effectively zero and the working capital cycle is far more cash-hungry than these targets imply. Establishing supplier credit terms is one of the direct financial benefits of building a proper supply chain (Chapter 2.4), alongside price and availability — a 30-day payables period on core purchases would fund a material part of the working capital requirement the franchisee currently carries in cash.
Payment discipline: supplier invoices must be paid on the due date — not before (which gives away free credit) and not after (which damages supplier relationships and may incur late-payment fees or loss of credit terms). A payment run schedule must be established (e.g. weekly on a fixed day) and adhered to.
Stock levels must be managed to minimise cash tied up in inventory without risking stock-outs. Par levels (see Chapter 5.3 — Ordering cycle & par levels) should be calibrated to the demand forecast (see Chapter 3.4 — Demand forecasting). Over-ordering ties up cash; under-ordering loses sales.
Card processor terms: verify and document the settlement terms with the card payment provider. If the processor holds funds for more than T+2, evaluate whether a better settlement arrangement is available. Even a one-day improvement in settlement terms can improve cash flow materially over a year.
VAT management: UK food service businesses charge different VAT rates depending on whether food is hot or cold and how it's sold (see VAT note above). The VAT collected belongs to HMRC and must be remitted per the VAT return period (typically quarterly, though smaller businesses can opt into the Annual Accounting Scheme). It must not be treated as available cash. Best practice: transfer the estimated VAT liability to a separate holding account after each VAT period to avoid a cash crunch at the payment deadline.
Working capital monitoring: the Franchise Owner must review the working capital position monthly as part of the monthly business review, covering: current stock value, outstanding supplier payables (total and ageing), card processor and delivery platform receivables in transit, and VAT liability accrued.
Roles & Responsibilities
Franchise Owner / Operator: Owns the working capital position; sets payment run schedule; manages the VAT holding account; reviews working capital monthly.
Unit Manager (currently Owner): Controls stock ordering to maintain target inventory levels; ensures supplier invoices are processed promptly; reports unusual cost items.
Head Office: Negotiates network-level supplier terms where applicable; provides working capital management guidance; benchmarks working capital performance across units.
External Accountant (if retained): Advises on VAT reporting and tax-efficient cash management; assists with annual working capital planning.
Measure Analyse Improve
Track monthly: (a) inventory holding period in days (stock value ÷ daily COGS), (b) average payables days (total payables ÷ daily purchases), (c) card/delivery-platform settlement lag (days from transaction to bank receipt), (d) VAT liability vs. VAT reserve balance. The goal is a negative working capital cycle (customers pay before suppliers are due) — normal for a QSR. If the cycle turns positive (you're paying out before cash comes in), investigate immediately.
Tools & Resources
Working capital dashboard (monthly — stock value, payables ageing, receivables in transit, VAT reserve). Payment run schedule (fixed day, weekly or bi-weekly). Supplier payment terms register (supplier name, agreed terms, actual average payment days). VAT holding account (separate bank account or ring-fenced within the main account). EPOS and stock management reports.

6.7 Franchisee performance remediation & exit
Underperformance early-warning indicators
Introduction
This chapter defines the financial and operational indicators that signal a unit is underperforming and may require formal intervention. Chapter 6.4 defines the criteria for growth; this chapter defines the mirror image — the criteria that trigger concern. Early-warning indicators are not punitive — they are diagnostic. The earlier a problem is identified, the more options exist to fix it. Left unaddressed, underperformance erodes franchisee viability, brand reputation, and network health.
Minimum Operating Standards
Financial early-warning indicators — any of the following triggers a formal review: (a) Revenue declining for 3 consecutive months on a like-for-like basis (adjusted for seasonality). (b) Net profit margin falling below the target set in Chapter 6.1 for 2 consecutive months; until that target is set, the indicator is net profit negative in any month, or declining for 2 consecutive months. (c) Food cost percentage exceeding target by more than 3 percentage points for 2 consecutive months. Transitional note: until the margin improvement programme is complete, this indicator is measured against the agreed improvement trajectory from the pricing workshop (Chapter 6.2 — Margin targets by product), not against the 25% end-state target — measured against the end state it would trigger continuously from the outset and cease to carry information. (d) Labour cost percentage exceeding target by more than 3 percentage points for 2 consecutive months — that is 27% against the current all-in target of 24%. The indicator references the target rather than a fixed number, so it moves if the target is revised. (e) Cash flow forecast projecting the cash balance falling below the minimum reserve (see Chapter 6.6) within the next 4 weeks.
Operational early-warning indicators — any of the following triggers a formal review: (a) Operational audit score falling below 80%, or declining by more than 10 percentage points between consecutive audits. This indicator is suspended until the internal audit framework exists (Chapter 6.1 — Core KPI definitions); it cannot be monitored against an audit that has not been designed. (b) Customer complaint rate exceeding 3 per 1,000 transactions for 2 consecutive months. (c) Staff turnover exceeding 70% annualised in any rolling 3-month period. (d) Food safety non-compliance identified during an Environmental Health inspection or an internal audit (immediate trigger — no waiting period). (e) Google review score declining below 4.3 — the minimum floor set in Chapter 4.3, not a lower warning level, because a threshold set below the standard means the unit is already failing the standard before anyone is warned — or declining by more than 0.3 points over a 3-month period.
When any early-warning indicator is triggered, the following process must be initiated: (1) Head Office issues a formal Underperformance Notice to the Franchise Owner, specifying which indicator(s) triggered the notice and the data behind it. (2) A review meeting is scheduled within 10 working days between the Franchise Owner, Unit Manager (currently Owner), and Head Office. (3) The review meeting must identify root causes, agree corrective actions, and set a remediation timeline. This feeds into the formal remediation process (see Chapter 6.7 — Formal remediation process & timeline).
Indicators must be monitored using the reporting cadences set out in Chapter 6.1 (Financial reporting & KPIs). Head Office is responsible for monitoring network-wide; the Franchise Owner is responsible for self-monitoring at unit level.
A single indicator triggering once may be explainable (a one-off event, a seasonal dip). The thresholds above are deliberately set at consecutive periods to filter out noise. However, a food safety non-compliance is always an immediate trigger regardless of pattern.
Roles & Responsibilities
Head Office: Monitors early-warning indicators across the network; issues Underperformance Notices; facilitates review meetings; tracks remediation.
Franchise Owner / Operator: Self-monitors indicators at unit level; responds to Underperformance Notices; participates in review meetings; implements corrective actions.
Unit Manager: Provides the operational data that feeds the indicators; participates in review meetings; executes corrective actions at unit level.
Measure Analyse Improve
Track: (a) number of Underperformance Notices issued per quarter, (b) time from indicator trigger to Notice issuance (target: within 5 working days of the data becoming available), (c) percentage of triggered units that resolve indicators within the agreed remediation timeline, (d) repeat triggers — units that resolve and then re-trigger within 6 months require deeper structural review.
Tools & Resources
Early-warning indicator dashboard (automated from EPOS, financial, and audit data where possible). Underperformance Notice template (formal letter: indicator triggered, supporting data, required actions, timeline). Review meeting agenda template. KPI definitions and targets (see Chapter 6.1). Audit scoring framework (see Chapter 6.5 — Field visit/audit cadence).
Formal remediation process & timeline
Introduction
This chapter defines the structured process for remediating underperformance at a Imran’s Diner unit once an early-warning indicator has been triggered and a review meeting has taken place. The remediation process is deliberately structured to mirror the readiness criteria in Chapter 6.4: just as expansion readiness is assessed against objective criteria with no partial credit, remediation is assessed against a formal improvement plan with defined milestones and a fixed timeline. The goal is always recovery — but the process must also create a clear, documented trail that supports escalation to franchise agreement default and termination if recovery does not materialise (see Chapter 6.7 — Franchise agreement default & termination conditions).
Minimum Operating Standards
Stage 1 — Improvement Plan (triggered by Underperformance Notice): Within 7 working days of the review meeting, Head Office and the Franchise Owner must jointly agree a written Improvement Plan. The plan must specify: (a) the specific indicators that triggered the process, (b) root causes identified, (c) corrective actions (specific, measurable, with named owners), (d) interim milestones (e.g. monthly checkpoints), (e) success criteria (the indicator thresholds that must be met for the remediation to be considered successful), and (f) the review period, which is 3 months.
Stage 2 — Monitored Improvement: During the review period, performance is tracked against the Improvement Plan milestones at the agreed frequency (typically monthly). Head Office provides support as agreed in the plan (additional training, operational visits, marketing support, etc.). The Franchise Owner must provide requested data and implement agreed actions within the stated timelines.
Stage 3 — Review: At the end of the review period, a formal assessment is conducted: (a) All indicators met — remediation successful; the unit exits the formal process. The case remains on file for 12 months for monitoring purposes, and a re-trigger within that window is treated under the deeper structural review provision in Chapter 6.6 rather than as a fresh first-time notice. (b) Partial improvement — indicators trending in the right direction but not yet met. Head Office may extend the review period by one further month, one extension only. (c) No improvement or deterioration — escalation to Stage 4.
Stage 4 — Final Notice: Head Office issues a Final Notice specifying: (a) which improvement criteria were not met, (b) what further action the Franchise Owner must take, (c) a final review date 30 days from the date of the Notice, and (d) the consequences of continued non-improvement (which may include franchise agreement default proceedings as defined in Chapter 6.7 — Franchise agreement default & termination conditions).
Documentation: every stage must be documented in writing, signed by both parties, and retained. This documentation is essential both for fairness (the franchisee must have clear notice of what is expected) and for legal enforceability if the process escalates to termination.
Support: the remediation process is not adversarial. Head Office must provide genuine, practical support during the improvement period — not just monitoring. If the Franchise Owner identifies a barrier that requires Head Office action (e.g. a supplier issue, a system deficiency, a brand-level marketing gap), Head Office must address it or acknowledge it as a mitigating factor.
Roles & Responsibilities
Head Office: Drafts the Improvement Plan (jointly with the Franchise Owner); provides support during the monitored period; conducts interim and final reviews; issues Final Notices; maintains documentation.
Franchise Owner / Operator: Participates in drafting the Improvement Plan; implements corrective actions; provides data and access for monitoring; escalates barriers to Head Office.
Unit Manager (currently Owner): Executes day-to-day corrective actions; provides operational data; participates in interim reviews.
Legal Advisor (if escalation to Stage 4): Reviews documentation trail; advises on franchise agreement implications; supports any formal dispute process.
Measure Analyse Improve
Track: (a) number of Improvement Plans initiated per year, (b) percentage resolved successfully at Stage 3 (target: majority — if most cases escalate to Stage 4, the support model may be inadequate), (c) average duration from Underperformance Notice to resolution, (d) number of cases escalated to Final Notice, (e) number of cases resulting in franchise agreement default proceedings. Review the remediation process itself annually — if it is not resulting in recoveries, the process or the support model needs adjustment.
Tools & Resources
Improvement Plan template (standardised format: indicators, root causes, corrective actions with owners and deadlines, milestones, success criteria, review dates). Interim review template (progress against each milestone). Final Notice template (formal letter). Case file (chronological documentation of all notices, meetings, plans, and reviews for each remediation case). Performance data (from EPOS, financial reports, audit scores, customer feedback — as per Chapter 6.1).

It cannot be safely templated. This is the ultimate backstop of the remediation process (Chapter 6.7) and must be drafted with legal review.
