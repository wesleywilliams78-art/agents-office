6.3 Performance review cadence
Weekly ops review
Introduction
This chapter defines the format and purpose of the weekly operational review meeting. The weekly ops review is the primary rhythm of operational management — it ensures the Franchisee and Unit Manager (currently Owner) are aligned on performance, priorities, and issues every week. Without a disciplined weekly cadence, problems compound and opportunities are missed.
Minimum Operating Standards

Territory/exclusivity policy
Introduction
This chapter defines Imran’s Diner's territory and exclusivity policy for franchise locations. Territory rights are one of the most important elements of a franchise agreement — they determine where a franchisee can and cannot operate, and whether the franchisor can place another unit nearby. Getting this wrong creates conflict, cannibalisation, and legal disputes.
Minimum Operating Standards
Each franchise location will be assigned a defined territory. The territory defines the geographic area within which: (a) the franchisee has the exclusive right to operate a Imran’s Diner unit, and (b) the franchisor will not open or grant another franchise within that area during the term of the agreement.
Territory definition method: territories are defined by whole postcode sectors (for example S2 4), not by a radius. A radius cuts through postcodes and leaves both parties arguing about which side of a line an address falls on; postcode sectors are unambiguous on the ground, map directly onto delivery platform zone configuration, and align with the population and competitor data used in site evaluation (Chapter 2.3). The territory map is maintained by Head Office and attached to each franchise agreement.
Exclusivity scope: the territory right protects against another physical Imran’s Diner unit within the defined area. It extends to delivery only in the ways competition law permits, and the distinction matters. Head Office configures and approves each unit's delivery zone on every platform, and a franchisee may not extend their platform delivery radius into another unit's postcode sectors without Head Office approval, nor actively market, advertise or run targeted promotions into another unit's territory. A franchisee is not prevented from fulfilling an unsolicited order placed by a customer located in another unit's territory. Under the Vertical Agreements Block Exemption Order 2022, restricting active sales into an exclusive territory is permissible, but restricting passive sales — responding to unsolicited customer requests — is a hardcore restriction that would remove block exemption protection from the franchise agreement as a whole. Operating a website or appearing on a delivery platform is a passive channel; targeted advertising into another's area is active. This distinction must be drafted explicitly into the franchise agreement, and the territorial and online-sales provisions reviewed by a competition lawyer before the agreement is issued.
Territory encroachment: if the franchisor wishes to open a unit that would overlap with an existing franchisee's territory, the existing franchisee must be given first right of refusal for the new location. If the franchisee declines, the franchisor may proceed, subject to the terms of the franchise agreement.
UK competition law note: the Chapter I prohibition of the Competition Act 1998 prohibits agreements that restrict competition. Territorial exclusivity clauses in franchise agreements are generally permissible under the Vertical Agreements Block Exemption Order 2022, provided the market share threshold is not exceeded and the clauses do not constitute hardcore restrictions. Legal review of the franchise agreement's territorial provisions is strongly recommended.
Territory review: territories may be reviewed and adjusted at franchise agreement renewal, by mutual agreement, if market conditions change materially (e.g. major residential or commercial development within the territory).
Roles & Responsibilities
Franchisor (Head Office): Defines territory boundaries; maintains the territory map; manages first-right-of-refusal processes; ensures competition law compliance.
Franchisee: Operates within the assigned territory; requests territory adjustments through the agreed process.
Legal Advisor: Reviews territorial provisions for competition law compliance.
Measure Analyse Improve
Track: (a) any territory disputes or complaints, (b) cannibalisation indicators (if two units are close, monitor whether one unit's sales decline when the other opens), (c) territory coverage vs. market opportunity (are there unserved areas?). Review territories as part of the annual planning process.
Tools & Resources

6.5 Franchisee support & escalation
Support model & SLAs
Introduction
This chapter defines the support model and service level agreements (SLAs) that Head Office provides to franchisees. As Imran’s Diner grows from a single owner-operated unit to a franchise network, the support function becomes critical — franchisees need to know what support is available, how to access it, and what response times to expect. Clear SLAs set expectations and hold both parties accountable.
Minimum Operating Standards
Support categories and SLAs: (a) Emergency (food safety incident, serious H&S event, regulatory authority contact, security incident) — response within 1 hour, at any time, including outside trading hours. Business hours are not the right frame for emergency response in this business: units trade to midnight and to 1am at weekends, and a refrigeration failure or a regulatory visit does not wait for 9am. (b) Urgent (equipment breakdown affecting trading, staffing crisis, significant customer complaint escalation) — response within 4 hours during trading hours — trading hours, not office hours. (c) Standard (operational queries, marketing requests, training support, reporting queries) — response within 2 business days. (d) Non-urgent (policy questions, process improvement suggestions, future planning) — response within 5 business days.
Support channels: emergency support is a named phone number, answered by a person — email-only is not acceptable for emergencies, and nor is a channel that routes to voicemail. Urgent, standard and non-urgent requests use a single logged channel: email to a monitored Head Office address until a ticketing system is adopted, so that every request is tracked against the SLA. Requests made informally by WhatsApp or direct message are not tracked and do not start the SLA clock; franchisees are told this at onboarding, because the most common cause of an unanswered support request is that it was never logged. Current position: Head Office is at present the Franchisee and Owner personally. These SLAs are the standard Head Office commits to and are drafted for the network at scale; they are not yet supported by a dedicated support function. Head Office resourcing must be reviewed before the second franchised unit opens, because an SLA the franchisor cannot meet damages the relationship more than an honest statement of current capacity would.
Head Office support scope includes: brand and marketing guidance, operational best practice, training materials and support, food safety and compliance guidance, financial reporting support, franchise agreement queries, and new unit setup support.
Head Office support does NOT include: day-to-day management of the unit (this is the franchisee's responsibility), HR case management (though guidance is available), legal advice (franchisees should retain their own legal advisor for unit-specific matters), and accounting/bookkeeping services.
Support request tracking: all support requests must be logged (by Head Office) with: request date, franchisee, category, description, response date, and resolution. This data is used to track SLA compliance and identify common support needs.
Franchisee obligations: to receive effective support, franchisees must provide timely and accurate information, follow up on recommendations, and maintain the systems and reporting required for Head Office to have visibility.

Escalation matrix
Introduction
This chapter provides the franchisee-to-Head Office escalation matrix — a structured framework for determining when and how issues should be escalated from the franchise unit to Head Office. This complements the internal unit escalation thresholds (Chapter 5.7) by defining the escalation path beyond the Franchisee.
Minimum Operating Standards
The escalation matrix operates on four levels: Level A (Unit-level resolution — Franchisee resolves without Head Office involvement): day-to-day operational issues, routine HR matters (within legal compliance), local marketing execution, minor equipment repairs, standard customer complaints.
Level B (Head Office informed — Franchisee resolves, Head Office is notified for awareness): staff turnover above target levels, customer complaints trending upward, minor non-compliance findings on internal audit, local competitor activity with potential brand impact, planned menu or pricing deviations from standard.
Level C (Head Office consulted — Franchisee seeks guidance before acting): dismissal or serious disciplinary action, any contact from regulatory authorities (Environmental Health, the HSE, Trading Standards, HMRC), customer complaint alleging food safety failure, lease renegotiation or property issues, any proposed change to unit layout, signage, or brand presentation, and any single item of capital expenditure above £2,500, or cumulative capital expenditure above £10,000 in any 12-month period. The cumulative test matters as much as the single-item one: a unit can re-equip itself piece by piece, each item below the threshold, and arrive somewhere Head Office never approved. Capital expenditure on equipment must in all cases be specified against the approved equipment list (Chapter 2.2), whatever its value. [Tawheed to confirm: whether £2,500 and £10,000 are the right thresholds — working figures pending the Site #2 capital requirement being established]
Level D (Head Office leads — Head Office takes the lead in resolution): media enquiries or negative press/social media crisis, legal claims or threats of litigation, franchise agreement disputes, food safety incidents requiring product recall or public communication, serious H&S incidents (RIDDOR-reportable), and any matter affecting the brand across multiple locations.
Response expectations: Level B notifications — Head Office acknowledges within 1 business day. Level C consultations — Head Office responds with guidance within the SLA for the relevant support category (see Support model & SLAs chapter). Level D matters — Head Office takes immediate ownership and communicates an action plan within 4 hours during trading hours, and within 12 hours in all cases. 24 hours is too slow for the matters Level D covers: a media enquiry has a deadline, and a social media crisis is substantially settled within a day, so an action plan that arrives the next morning arrives after the narrative has formed. The initial communication does not have to be the resolution — it has to be the holding position and the named owner.
Documentation: all Level C and D escalations must be documented with the same rigour as internal escalations (date, issue, who was notified, actions taken, outcome).
Roles & Responsibilities
Franchisee: Classifies issues against the matrix; notifies or consults Head Office as required; retains responsibility for unit-level matters.
Unit Manager (currently Owner): Provides information and supports the Franchisee in escalation; does not escalate directly to Head Office (routes through Franchisee).
Head Office: Responds within SLA; leads on Level D matters; provides guidance on Level C; monitors Level B notifications for patterns.
Measure Analyse Improve
Track: (a) escalation volume by level, (b) appropriateness of classification (was the right level selected?), (c) Head Office response times vs. SLA, (d) outcomes and resolution effectiveness. Review quarterly; adjust the matrix as the franchise network matures.
Tools & Resources
Escalation matrix reference document (one-page summary for Franchisee reference). Escalation log. Support request tracking system (shared with the Support model & SLAs chapter). Level C/D escalation report template.

6.6 Cash flow & capital governance
Cash flow forecasting & monitoring
Introduction
This chapter defines the standard process for forecasting, monitoring, and managing cash flow at unit and entity level. A profitable unit can still fail if it runs out of cash — profit is an accounting concept; cash is what pays the bills. This is the foundation that Chapter 6.4 (Readiness criteria for site #2) assumes exists: the requirement that expansion capital be secured 'without jeopardising Site #1's cash position' is meaningless without a disciplined cash flow forecast and monitoring cadence.
Minimum Operating Standards
A rolling 13-week cash flow forecast must be maintained by the Franchise Owner (or their finance lead) and updated weekly. The 13-week horizon covers one full quarter and is the standard minimum for operational cash management in SMEs.
The forecast must be structured as a weekly cash-in / cash-out matrix covering at minimum: (a) Cash in — daily/weekly sales receipts (from EPOS), other income (e.g. catering, delivery platform payouts, events). (b) Cash out — supplier payments (by supplier, by due date), payroll and employer costs (including Employer's Class 1 National Insurance Contributions), rent, utilities, loan/lease repayments, VAT payments (VAT on hot takeaway food is standard-rated at 20%), Corporation Tax/Self Assessment payments on account, insurance premiums, marketing spend, maintenance/repairs, and any one-off capex. (c) Net cash flow per week and cumulative cash balance.
The forecast must be reconciled against the actual bank balance weekly. Any variance exceeding £1,000 or 5% of forecast, whichever is lower, must be investigated and the forecast adjusted. Both tests apply and the tighter one binds: a percentage alone lets a large absolute variance pass in a strong week, and a fixed sum alone becomes meaningless as revenue grows. A variance that recurs in the same direction for three consecutive weeks is a forecasting failure rather than a timing difference, and the forecast is rebuilt rather than adjusted.
A minimum cash reserve — the lowest acceptable bank balance — must be defined and maintained, set at 8 weeks of fixed costs, not 4. Eight rather than four, for three reasons particular to this business: trade is highly seasonal, with December and Ramadan both moving cash materially and Ramadan inverting the daily pattern for a month; the unit is single-sourced on meat with no approved backup (Chapter 5.7 — Supply disruption contingency), so a supply failure is a menu reduction or a closure rather than a switch of supplier; and the business carries a known VAT configuration error (Chapter 6.2 — Pricing review process), so until that is quantified there is an unmeasured liability for which a four-week reserve leaves no room. Fixed costs for this purpose means rent, rates, utilities, insurance, loan and lease repayments, software subscriptions and the minimum staffing cost required to open the doors — not total costs, and not costs that fall away if the unit stops trading. [Tawheed to confirm: the fixed cost figure per week, from which the reserve is calculated] If the forecast projects the cash balance falling below the minimum reserve at any point in the 13-week horizon, the Franchise Owner must take corrective action immediately (defer discretionary spend, accelerate receivables, draw on credit facility if available), and the projection is escalated to Head Office as a Level C matter (Chapter 6.5 — Escalation matrix). A franchisee heading towards a cash problem is the one least likely to raise it, so the trigger is the forecast rather than the franchisee's judgement.
Cash flow reporting: a cash flow summary must be included in the monthly business review (see Chapter 6.3 — Monthly business review) covering: opening balance, total receipts, total payments, closing balance, and variance vs. forecast.
Before any capital expenditure commitment (see Chapter 6.6 — Capex authorisation framework), the Franchise Owner must demonstrate via the cash flow forecast that the expenditure can be absorbed without breaching the minimum cash reserve for the following 13 weeks.
Seasonal cash flow planning: once a full year of trading data is available, the forecast must incorporate known seasonal patterns (e.g. lower revenue in January, higher costs in December, annual insurance premium, quarterly VAT payments). Head Office should provide a seasonal cash flow adjustment template based on network-wide patterns.
Roles & Responsibilities
Franchise Owner / Operator: Owns the cash flow forecast; updates it weekly; takes corrective action when the reserve threshold is threatened; presents cash flow position at the monthly business review.
Unit Manager (currently Owner): Provides the revenue and operational cost data that feeds the forecast (daily sales, supplier invoices received, maintenance costs incurred).
Head Office: Provides the forecasting template; reviews cash flow position as part of the monthly business review; flags network-wide cash flow risks (e.g. supplier price increases, regulatory cost changes).
External Accountant (if retained): Assists with tax instalment calculations, VAT timing, and annual cash flow planning.
Measure Analyse Improve
Track: (a) forecast accuracy — actual vs. predicted cash balance, weekly and monthly, (b) number of weeks the cash balance fell below the minimum reserve, (c) average days' cash on hand (closing balance ÷ average daily operating cost), (d) variance between forecast and actual on each major cost line. A forecast that is consistently inaccurate in one direction (e.g. always overestimates receipts) indicates a systematic bias that must be corrected.
