1.1  Brand story & vision

Origin story & founder journey  [YSF ADVISORY — FINAL DRAFT – SUBJECT TO ADNAN SIGN-OFF]

Introduction
Every franchise system begins with a founding story — the original insight, the early risks, and the lessons that shaped the brand before it became a replicable model. This chapter documents Creamy's origin so that every franchisee, manager, and team member understands where the brand came from, what it survived, and why it operates the way it does today.
This is not decorative history. The founding story carries operational weight: it explains the reasoning behind standards that might otherwise seem arbitrary (why Creamy avoids shopping centres, why portion sizes are non-negotiable, why product originality is a brand principle). A franchisee who understands the origin is better equipped to make decisions that are consistent with the brand — even in situations the manual does not explicitly cover.

Minimum Operating Standards
The following founding narrative must be communicated to every franchisee during onboarding and made available to all team members. It is not optional brand storytelling — it is the operational context behind the standards in this manual.
The idea (2010–2014). Creamy began with a question. Between 2010 and 2012, Adnan Tasleem visited London regularly and noticed a category of restaurant that did not exist in Norway: dedicated dessert restaurants where people went specifically to sit down and enjoy desserts as the main event, not as an afterthought to a savoury meal. The thought was simple — "Why don't we have this in Norway?"
The original plan was to franchise an established UK dessert brand. Around 2014, Adnan approached a UK-based dessert chain about bringing their concept to Norway. The franchise fee was unaffordable at the time. Rather than abandoning the idea, Adnan decided to build his own concept from scratch — no franchise playbook, no industry background, no restaurant experience.
Self-taught foundations. Adnan had no formal training in food preparation, hospitality, or business management. Core skills — including how to make crepes — were learned from YouTube tutorials and personal experimentation. This self-taught approach is relevant context: the systems and standards documented in this manual exist precisely because the founder learned first-hand what happens when you operate without them.
Opening (2016). Creamy opened its first location in Greenland, Oslo, in 2016. The premises were a commercial property owned by Adnan's father, who also provided the initial loan to fund the business. Every piece of equipment — fridges, freezers, the coffee machine — was purchased secondhand. The interior was fitted out in what became Creamy's signature aesthetic: pink walls, black chairs and tables.
For the first year, the operation consisted of Adnan and one part-time employee. Adnan worked every day with no days off.
The survival test (Winter 2016/17). In its first winter, Creamy experienced a near-total drop in sales. Dessert demand in Norway is heavily seasonal, and the winter months tested whether the concept was viable at all. Adnan seriously considered closing the business and told his father he wanted to quit. His father's response — "Hold out. It will turn" — kept the operation alive through the low season.
The turning point (Spring 2017). In March or April 2017, Adnan changed the waffle recipe to a golden waffle mix. The product improvement coincided with the seasonal upturn and sales recovered. The golden waffle remains a core menu item today. This episode established a principle that now runs through Creamy's operations: when sales are down, improve the product — do not discount it.
Brand recognition. By approximately 2017, Creamy had become a recognised local landmark. A defining moment: a customer on the phone told the person they were meeting, "I'm at Creamy, just come here" — no address needed. The brand had become a navigation point. This is the level of local embeddedness every Creamy location should aim for.
The shopping centre lesson (2018). In 2018, Creamy opened a second unit inside a shopping centre. It was, in Adnan's words, "a disaster." The location was sold after approximately one year at a financial loss. The experience produced a firm operational rule: Creamy does not operate in shopping centres. This is not a preference — it is a tested conclusion based on direct experience, and it is reflected in the site selection criteria elsewhere in this manual (see Chapter 2.2 — Site evaluation criteria).
Rebuilding and growth (2019–present). After closing the shopping centre unit, Adnan refocused entirely on the original Greenland location. In 2020, a second Greenland location was opened and remains operational. In 2023, the search began for a third site. In summer 2025, the Stranden location was identified. It opened in March 2026 and represents the current benchmark for how a Creamy unit should look and operate — purpose-built to the brand's evolved standards, fitted with professional-grade equipment, and designed from the ground up rather than adapted from secondhand parts.
What the founder wants every franchisee to know. These principles emerged from lived experience, not theory. They are embedded throughout this manual, but their origin is here:
Quality over margin. "What we serve is something we would like to be served." Portion sizes are generous by design. The instinct to reduce portions to protect margin runs against Creamy's identity.
Originality as brand discipline. "If somebody else is doing it, I don't believe we should do it." Creamy positions itself as a first-mover in the Norwegian dessert market. Copying competitor products or trends is a brand violation, not a shortcut.
The "happy place" standard. Creamy should feel like a place where people treat themselves — "giving yourself a luxury in an everyday normal life." This applies to environment, service, and product presentation alike.
Family roots. Creamy was built with family support — a father's property, a father's loan, a father's advice at the lowest point. The brand values reflect that: warmth, generosity, loyalty.

Roles & Responsibilities
Franchise Owner / Operator: Must ensure that every team member has been introduced to the founding story during onboarding. The origin story should be referenced when explaining brand standards that stem from it (e.g. portion policy, site selection, product originality).
Unit Manager: Reinforces founding principles in day-to-day operations. When correcting a team member on a brand standard, connecting the correction to the "why" from the origin story is more effective than citing the rule alone.
Team Members: Expected to understand the brand's origin at a level sufficient to answer a customer who asks, "How did Creamy start?" A team member who can tell the story authentically is a brand asset.
Creamy Head Office: Maintains the authoritative version of the founding narrative. Any public-facing use of the origin story (press, social media, franchise recruitment materials) must be consistent with this chapter.

Measure, Analyse, Improve
The origin story is a cultural asset, not a metric. It is not measured in the same way as operational KPIs. However, the following should be assessed:
(a) Onboarding coverage: Every new starter should be able to summarise Creamy's founding story within their first week. This can be assessed informally during onboarding check-ins (see Chapter 3.1 — Onboarding checklist).
(b) Brand consistency in communication: Monitor external communications (social media, press, franchise recruitment materials) for consistency with the origin narrative as documented here. Any embellishment, factual error, or deviation should be corrected at source.
(c) Team alignment: During periodic team meetings or reviews, the Unit Manager should assess whether team members understand why key brand standards exist (e.g. "Why doesn't Creamy open in shopping centres?" "Why are portions the size they are?"). Inability to connect standards to their origin may indicate the story is not being communicated effectively during onboarding.

Introduction
A franchise system without a clearly articulated vision is a collection of units without direction. This chapter documents Creamy's long-term ambition as defined by the founder, so that every franchisee, manager, and team member understands where the brand is heading and what their individual unit contributes to that trajectory.
Vision in a franchise context is not aspirational marketing copy — it is the strategic frame that governs site selection priorities, franchise recruitment pace, brand investment decisions, and the operational standards required to support growth without diluting quality. A franchisee who understands the growth horizon can plan their own business accordingly; a team member who understands the brand ambition can represent it with conviction.
This chapter should be read alongside Chapter 1.1 (Origin Story & Founder Journey), which explains where Creamy came from, and Chapter 1.3 (Positioning vs. category), which defines what Creamy is in the market.

Minimum Operating Standards
The following vision statement and growth plan represent the founder's stated ambition for Creamy. They must be communicated to every franchisee at the point of franchise agreement and referenced during annual business planning.
Brand vision. Creamy will be recognised as Norway's leading modern, high-quality dessert brand — a brand that is always one step ahead of competitors, never following. This is not a tagline; it is an operational commitment that shapes product development, store design, and market positioning across every location.
The vision extends beyond Norway. Creamy's long-term ambition is to become a Scandinavian brand, beginning with full coverage of the Norwegian market and expanding into Sweden.

Positioning vs. category  [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW - COMPLETED FROM VOICE NOTE]

Introduction
This chapter defines what Creamy is in the market — the category it operates in, how it is positioned relative to competitors, and the deliberate strategic choices that distinguish it from other brands. Positioning is not a marketing exercise alone; it determines pricing logic, product development boundaries, store design standards, recruitment messaging, and how every team member describes Creamy to a customer.
For a franchise system, positioning clarity is particularly critical. Without it, individual franchisees will default to their own interpretation of what the brand is, leading to inconsistency across locations. This chapter provides the authoritative answer.

Minimum Operating Standards
Category definition. Creamy operates in the Dessert Bar category. It is not a café that happens to serve desserts, not a bakery, not an ice cream parlour, and not a restaurant with a dessert menu. It is a dedicated dessert destination — a place customers visit specifically to sit down and enjoy high-quality desserts as the primary experience.
This category definition must be used consistently in all franchise documentation, marketing materials, press communications, and any context where Creamy is asked to describe what it is.
Market position. Creamy holds a strong market position in Norway: it is the only dessert brand operating multiple locations in the Norwegian market. There is currently no direct domestic competitor with a comparable multi-unit dessert bar model. This first-mover position is a strategic asset that the growth roadmap (see Chapter 1.2 — Vision & Long-Term Ambition) is designed to consolidate before competitors enter the space.
The absence of direct multi-unit competition does not mean there is no competitive landscape. Creamy competes for customer spend with cafés, bakeries, ice cream chains, and QSR brands that offer desserts as part of a broader menu. The distinction — and Creamy's advantage — is specialism: a dedicated dessert bar offers a depth of product, an environment, and an experience that generalist competitors cannot match as a sideline.
Differentiation from international dessert brands. Creamy's original concept was inspired by the UK dessert restaurant category, which Adnan observed during visits to London between 2010 and 2012 (see Chapter 1.1 — Origin Story). However, from the point of opening in 2016, Creamy deliberately ceased following any specific international brand and began building its own identity. This was a conscious strategic decision, not a gradual drift.
The founder's rationale: Creamy must not be perceived as a look-alike of any existing international dessert brand. Being seen as a copy — even an unofficial one — would undermine the brand's credibility, its "one step ahead" positioning (see Chapter 1.2), and its ability to command customer loyalty on its own merits.
In practice, this means:
Product inspiration is drawn globally, not from a single source. Creamy looks to dessert concepts and ingredients from around the world to develop its menu. It does not track or replicate the menu of any single competitor or international brand.
No brand association with international dessert chains. Creamy must never be described as "the Norwegian version of [X]" or "like [X] but in Norway" in any official communication — marketing materials, press responses, franchise recruitment, social media, or team member descriptions to customers. If a customer or journalist makes the comparison, the response should acknowledge the broader dessert bar category without accepting the specific brand association. Suggested framing: "There are dessert brands in other countries, but Creamy is a Norwegian-built concept — we've developed our own menu and identity from the ground up."
Visual and operational distinctiveness. Store design, branding, menu format, and product presentation must be demonstrably Creamy's own. Any proposed product, design element, or marketing approach that closely resembles a specific international competitor should be rejected or reworked to ensure clear differentiation. This applies to franchisees proposing new ideas as much as to Head Office development.
The originality principle. The positioning decision reinforces the originality principle stated in Chapter 1.1 and Chapter 1.2: Creamy leads, it does not follow. This applies not only to international brands but to domestic competitors. If another operator in the Norwegian market launches a product or concept, Creamy's response is not to copy it but to assess whether it represents a trend worth leading on with Creamy's own interpretation, or a direction that does not fit the brand.
Franchisees must understand this as a non-negotiable brand discipline. Proposing a product "because [competitor] is doing well with it" is not a valid rationale within Creamy's operating model.

Roles & Responsibilities
Founder / Creamy Head Office: Owns the positioning. Any change to category definition, competitive stance, or differentiation strategy is a Head Office decision, not a franchisee-level choice. Ensures all franchise recruitment, marketing, and public communications are consistent with this chapter. Monitors the competitive landscape for emerging threats to the first-mover position.
Franchise Owner / Operator: Operates within the positioning as defined. Does not reposition the brand locally (e.g. by describing the unit as a "café" or "restaurant" rather than a dessert bar). Ensures that local marketing and customer communications use the approved category and positioning language. Flags any local competitive developments to Head Office.

Measure, Analyse, Improve
(a) Positioning consistency audit: During operational audits and franchisee reviews, check that the unit's local marketing, in-store signage, and team language are consistent with the positioning defined in this chapter. Any instance of the brand being described as a café, restaurant, or as a variant of another brand should be corrected immediately.
(b) Competitive landscape monitoring: Head Office should conduct a quarterly scan of the Norwegian dessert market. Key questions: Has a new multi-unit dessert concept entered the market? Are existing competitors (cafés, bakeries, QSR) increasing their dessert focus in a way that encroaches on Creamy's positioning? Is any brand explicitly copying Creamy's concept or products?
(c) Customer perception check: As the estate grows, include a positioning question in any customer feedback mechanism: "How would you describe Creamy to a friend?" Responses that align with "dessert bar," "dessert place," or "treat/luxury" indicate healthy positioning. Responses that name a competitor or describe Creamy as a café indicate a perception gap.
(d) Brand trajectory indicator: Track the Google review score trend over a rolling 6-month period (see Chapter 6.1 — KPI dashboard design and Chapter 4.3 — Review/reputation management). A consistently high and improving score, combined with review language that reflects the intended positioning, confirms the brand is landing as intended.

1.2  Values

Core values statement   [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW - COMPLETED FROM VOICE NOTE]

Introduction
This chapter defines Creamy's core values — the non-negotiable principles that govern how every person in the organisation works, makes decisions, and represents the brand. Values in a franchise system are not motivational wall art. They are decision-making tools: when a team member faces a choice the manual does not explicitly cover, the values tell them which way to lean.
Creamy's values were not designed by a branding agency. They emerged from the founder's lived experience building the business from a single unit with secondhand equipment and no days off (see Chapter 1.1 — Origin Story & Founder Journey). That origin gives them weight — they are tested principles, not corporate aspirations.
This chapter states the values and what each one means operationally. Chapter 1.5 (Values in Action) translates them into observable daily behaviours across every role.

Minimum Operating Standards
Creamy operates by four core values. They are listed in the order the founder articulated them, and that order is intentional — quality comes first because everything else depends on it.
1. Quality Always First
The product wins when the quality is high. This is Creamy's foundational belief: if the product is excellent, customers come back; if it is not, nothing else — not marketing, not location, not pricing — compensates.
What this means in practice:
Product quality is never traded off against speed, cost, or convenience. If a product cannot be served to the required standard, it is not served. A short-term saving that reduces product quality is not a saving — it is brand damage.
Ingredients, preparation methods, and presentation standards exist for a reason. Deviating from them to save time or reduce waste is a values violation, not an operational shortcut.
"Quality" applies beyond the product itself — to the cleanliness of the unit, the state of the equipment, the presentation of the serving area, and the appearance of the team. A high-quality dessert served in a low-quality environment undermines the value.
When something goes wrong with a product (a bad batch, a supply issue, an equipment failure), the default response is to stop serving that product until the quality can be restored — not to serve a compromised version.
This value connects directly to operational standards throughout the manual: recipe adherence (Chapter 5.5), food safety (Chapter 3.6), and the "one step ahead" positioning principle (Chapter 1.3 — Positioning vs. Category).
2. Generous Portions
Customers appreciate being filled up with their favourite dessert. Generous portions are not a promotional tactic or a temporary differentiator — they are a permanent brand commitment.
What this means in practice:
Portion sizes are defined in the recipe and product specifications. They are minimums, not targets. A portion that looks small relative to the specification must be corrected.
The instinct to reduce portions to protect food cost percentage is understood but rejected as a strategy. Creamy's commercial model is built around the margin that generous portions at the right price point deliver — not around portion control as a cost lever. (See Chapter 6.2 — Pricing review framework for how pricing should absorb food cost rather than portion reduction.)
Team members should never feel they need to be stingy with product. If food cost is running high, the issue is waste, purchasing, or pricing — not the amount of product reaching the customer.
"Generous" does not mean "uncontrolled." Portions must be consistent — a customer should receive the same amount on their tenth visit as their first. Consistency is part of generosity; it means the customer can trust what they are getting.
3. Never Give Up
The founder built Creamy through a winter with near-zero sales, a failed shopping centre location, and years of working every day without a break. The business exists because he did not quit. That resilience is a value the brand passes on to every person who works in it.
What this means in practice:
Problems have solutions. The default mindset at every level — from team member to franchisee — is that obstacles are worked through, not accepted as permanent. A quiet Tuesday, a bad review, a staffing shortage, a failed product trial: these are situations to solve, not reasons to lower standards.
Leadership at Creamy is built on encouragement, not pressure. The founder's own leadership style — going around the business praising employees for their work — sets the tone. Managers and franchisees should be the people in the unit who recognise effort, celebrate progress, and remind the team that everything has a solution.
This value is not about refusing to acknowledge when something is genuinely wrong. It is about the response: diagnose the problem, find the fix, execute it. The origin story (Chapter 1.1) is the proof that this approach works — the product improvement that turned the first winter around was a solution found under pressure, not a lucky break.
For franchisees specifically: building a new Creamy location is hard. The first months will test resolve. This value means Creamy Head Office supports franchisees through difficult early trading — but it also means franchisees are expected to bring the same persistence the founder demonstrated, not to look for an exit at the first downturn.
4. Create Happiness
Customers come to Creamy not because they have to, but because they have made a choice to give themselves something extra special that day. Creamy's job is to make sure that choice is rewarded.
What this means in practice:
Every customer interaction starts from the recognition that this visit is a treat, not a routine errand. The customer has chosen to do something nice for themselves — or for the people they are with. The team's role is to make that moment land.
"Happiness" is created through the combination of product, environment, and service. A great dessert served by an indifferent team member in a messy unit does not create happiness. All three elements must be present.
The standard is not "adequate service" — it is warmth. Customers should feel welcomed, not processed. This does not require scripts or forced enthusiasm; it requires team members who understand that they are part of someone's good moment and act accordingly.
This value applies to how the team treats each other, not only customers. A team that enjoys working together creates a better atmosphere than one that merely functions. Managers should actively foster a positive working environment — not because it is a nice idea, but because customers can tell the difference.
Values hierarchy. In situations where values appear to conflict, the ordering applies. Quality comes first — a unit should never compromise product quality in pursuit of speed, even if a customer is waiting. Generous portions come second — do not reduce a portion to make a product look more "refined." Never give up and create happiness guide behaviour and attitude, and in practice they reinforce each other: a team that believes problems can be solved is a team that creates a better customer experience.

Roles & Responsibilities
Founder / Creamy Head Office: Owns the values. Ensures they are embedded in franchise agreements, training programmes, marketing, and operational standards. Models the values in all interactions with the franchise network. Does not dilute, rebrand, or add to the values without careful consideration — four values are manageable; ten are wallpaper.
Franchise Owner / Operator: Lives the values visibly. A franchisee who talks about quality but cuts portions, or who preaches "never give up" but blames the team when things go wrong, undermines the entire framework. The franchisee is the local embodiment of the values — their behaviour sets the standard for their unit.
Unit Manager: Translates values into daily management. Praises team members (never give up / create happiness). Holds the line on product and portion standards (quality always first / generous portions). Addresses values misalignment promptly — a team member who is not creating happiness for customers needs coaching, not just a performance note.
Team Members: Understand and apply the values in every shift. Can articulate what the values are if asked. Recognise that the values are not abstract principles — they are the reason Creamy exists and the reason customers come back.

Measure, Analyse, Improve
Values are measured through behaviour, not declarations. The following indicators assess whether the values are being lived:
(a) Quality always first: Product consistency scores during audits; customer complaints related to product quality; instances of substandard product being served (tracked through waste logs and customer feedback). A unit with high quality scores is living this value. A unit with recurring quality complaints is not, regardless of what the manager says in meetings.
(b) Generous portions: Portion compliance during audits (are portions meeting specifications?); customer feedback mentioning portion size (positive mentions confirm the value is landing; complaints suggest drift). Food cost percentage should be monitored but interpreted in context — a rising food cost that correlates with strong sales and positive portion feedback may be the value working as intended.
(c) Never give up: Staff retention and morale indicators; how the unit responds to operational setbacks (does the team problem-solve or disengage?); franchisee engagement during difficult trading periods. This is harder to quantify — the best measure is whether the unit's culture visibly embodies resilience and encouragement.
(d) Create happiness: Customer review sentiment (see Chapter 4.3 — Review/reputation management); mystery diner or informal observation of team-customer interactions; team satisfaction (a happy team is a leading indicator of happy customers). Look for language in reviews that reflects the value: "welcoming," "made our day," "felt special," "great atmosphere."

Introduction
Chapter 1.4 defines Creamy's four core values: Quality Always First, Generous Portions, Never Give Up, and Create Happiness. This chapter translates those values into observable, daily behaviours — what living the values actually looks like on shift, in a management meeting, and across the franchise relationship.
Values that remain abstract are values that get ignored. A team member who knows the value is "Quality Always First" but has never been shown what that means when a dessert comes out wrong, when a customer complains, or when a shortcut is available will default to their own judgement in the moment. This chapter removes that ambiguity by providing concrete behavioural standards for every role.
This chapter should be used during onboarding (see Chapter 3.1 — Onboarding checklist), referenced in team briefings, and applied during performance reviews and operational audits.

Minimum Operating Standards
The following behavioural standards describe how Creamy's four core values are demonstrated in practice. They are grouped by value, with specific expectations for each role level.

Quality Always First — in action
The founding principle: "We will win when the quality of the product is high."
Team Members:
Check every dessert against the product specification before it leaves the kitchen. If it does not meet the standard — presentation, portion, temperature, freshness — remake it. Do not serve a product you would not want to be served yourself.
When a customer complains about a product, remake the dessert. Do not argue, do not offer a partial fix, do not seek manager approval first. The cost of remaking one dessert is less than the cost of a bad review. This is a standing instruction from the founder.
Report any ingredient that looks, smells, or performs differently from normal — even if it is within its use-by date. Quality problems start in the prep, not at the pass.
Keep your station clean throughout service, not just at close. A clean station produces better product and catches problems earlier.
Unit Manager:
Conduct a visual quality check at least once per service period — walk the pass and assess what is going out. If standards are slipping, intervene immediately rather than noting it for later.
When you see a team member remake a substandard product without being told, recognise it. That behaviour is exactly what this value requires and reinforcing it is how it becomes culture.
Track remakes and complaints not as failures but as quality data. A rising remake count may indicate a training gap, a supplier issue, or an equipment problem — diagnose the cause rather than pressuring the team to stop remaking.
Never instruct or imply that a team member should serve a product they have flagged as below standard. Overriding a quality call to protect speed or waste targets directly contradicts this value.
Franchise Owner / Operator:
Invest in equipment, ingredients, and training that support product quality. Cutting supplier quality to improve food cost percentage is a short-term gain that erodes the brand's foundational value.
Review quality-related customer feedback monthly (see Chapter 4.3 — Review/reputation management). Persistent quality complaints at your unit are a values failure, not just an operational one.
Apply the founder's standing rule without exception: a remade dessert costs less than a bad review. If your unit is not remaking products when they fall short, your team either does not know the rule or does not believe you support it. Both are fixable.

Generous Portions — in action
The founding principle: "People appreciate getting filled up with their favourite dessert."
Team Members:
Follow the portion specification for every product. The specification is the minimum — do not portion below it. Consistency matters: a customer who receives a visibly smaller portion than last time notices, and it damages trust.
If you are unsure whether a portion looks right, ask. It is better to check than to send out something that looks mean on the plate.
Do not adjust portions based on how busy the shift is or how much stock is left. If stock is running low on an item, communicate it to the manager so a decision can be made about availability — do not silently reduce portions to stretch supply.
Unit Manager:
Spot-check portions during service. Use a scale if product specifications include weights. Visual checks are acceptable for items where presentation defines the portion, but weigh-checks should be periodic.
If food cost is above target, investigate waste, prep efficiency, and purchasing before looking at portion sizes. Portions are not the cost lever in Creamy's model — they are the value proposition. (See Chapter 6.2 — Pricing review framework.)
Brief the team on what "generous" looks like for each product, ideally with a visual reference. A photo of the correct portion for each menu item, displayed in the kitchen, is more effective than a number on a spec sheet.
Franchise Owner / Operator:
Understand that generous portions are a brand commitment, not a variable you control locally. A franchisee who reduces portions to improve unit-level margin is working against the brand and will be identified through audit and customer feedback.
Price to accommodate generous portions, not the other way around. If margin is under pressure, the pricing conversation comes before the portion conversation — always.

Never Give Up — in action
The founding principle: "Everything has a solution."
Team Members:
Making a mistake in the kitchen is never a problem. What matters is what happens next: be brave enough to own the mistake, tell your manager, and help fix it. This is a direct instruction from the founder. A mistake that is admitted is a mistake that gets solved quickly. A mistake that is hidden wastes everyone's time — the manager's time investigating, the team's time compensating, and potentially the customer's experience.
When a shift is difficult — understaffed, unexpectedly busy, equipment playing up — the expectation is not that you handle it perfectly. The expectation is that you keep going, communicate what is happening, and ask for help when you need it.
If you see a problem that you do not know how to fix, say so. "I don't know how to fix this but it needs fixing" is a valuable contribution, not an admission of failure.
Unit Manager:
Your primary leadership tool is encouragement, not correction. The founder's own approach — going around the business praising employees for their work — is the model. Catch people doing things right and say so. This is not soft management; it is how you build a team that stays, improves, and takes ownership.
