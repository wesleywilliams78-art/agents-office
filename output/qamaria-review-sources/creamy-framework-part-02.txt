When a team member admits a mistake, your first response sets the culture. Thank them for telling you, fix the problem together, and use it as a coaching moment. If your response is punitive — even through tone or body language — the next mistake will be hidden, and hidden mistakes are the ones that reach customers.
When the unit faces a setback — a bad trading week, a negative review, a failed audit point — frame it as a problem to solve, not a failure to assign blame for. Walk the team through the solution, not just the diagnosis.
Protect your own resilience. Managing a QSR unit is demanding, and "never give up" applies to your own career trajectory. Use the support structures available (see Chapter 1.6 — Who to contact with questions) and raise issues with the franchisee or Head Office before they become crises.
Franchise Owner / Operator:
The early months of a new Creamy location will test your resolve (see Chapter 1.1 — Origin Story: the founder's first winter). This is expected, not exceptional. Creamy Head Office provides support during the launch period (see Chapter 2.4 — Opening support), but the resilience must come from the franchisee.
Build a culture where problems are surfaced, not buried. A unit where the manager is afraid to report bad news to the franchisee is a unit where problems compound. Your role is to make it safe to escalate — and then to act on what is escalated.

Create Happiness — in action
The founding principle: "They come to Creamy not because they have to, but because they have made a choice to give themselves something extra special that day."
Team Members:
Every customer walking through the door has chosen to be here. They chose Creamy over staying home, over going somewhere else, over saving the money. Your job is to make that choice feel right — from the greeting to the goodbye.
Warmth is not a script. You do not need to follow a scripted greeting or a forced upsell sequence. You need to be genuinely welcoming, attentive, and present. If a customer is celebrating, acknowledge it. If a child is excited about their dessert, share the moment. If someone is having a quiet treat alone, give them space but let them know you are there.
The environment is part of the happiness. A clean table, good music at the right volume, a well-lit and well-presented serving area — these are not housekeeping tasks, they are experience standards.
If something goes wrong during a customer's visit — a long wait, a product issue, a spill — recover it with warmth, not just efficiency. A remade dessert delivered with a genuine apology and a smile turns a complaint into a story the customer tells in the brand's favour.
Unit Manager:
Create happiness starts with the team. A team that dreads coming to work does not create happiness for customers — it is that simple. Your role is to build a shift environment where people want to be, where effort is recognised, and where the team supports each other.
Monitor the energy of the unit, not just the operations. If the atmosphere feels flat — music off, team disengaged, no conversation at the counter — that is an operational issue, not an intangible. Diagnose it and fix it the same way you would fix a broken freezer.
Recognise team members who create standout customer moments. Share those moments in team briefings. What gets recognised gets repeated.
Franchise Owner / Operator:
Invest in the elements that create happiness: the environment (maintenance, décor, lighting, music), the team (fair wages, good scheduling, recognition), and the product (see Quality Always First). Underinvesting in any of these and expecting the team to compensate with personality is not sustainable.
Read your customer reviews through the happiness lens (see Chapter 4.3 — Review/reputation management). Reviews that mention the atmosphere, the service, and the feeling of the visit — not just the food — indicate this value is landing. Reviews that mention only the product, or that describe the experience as transactional, suggest the happiness layer is missing.

Cross-cutting behavioural standard: the remake rule
One behaviour captures multiple values at once and deserves its own emphasis because it is a daily, concrete decision point:
When a customer complains about a product, remake it. Immediately. Without debate.
This is not a guideline — it is a standing operational instruction from the founder. The rationale spans three of the four values: Quality Always First (the product was not right, so fix it), Create Happiness (the customer chose to be here — recover their experience), and Never Give Up (a complaint is a problem to solve, not a loss to accept). The cost of a remade dessert is a fraction of the cost of a bad Google review, a lost repeat customer, or a social media post about a poor experience.
Team members must be empowered to remake without seeking approval. If a manager has to be consulted before a remake happens, the process is too slow and the message to the team is wrong — it says "we don't trust your judgement on quality," which contradicts the values entirely.

Roles & Responsibilities
Founder / Creamy Head Office: Sets the behavioural standards as documented in this chapter. Models the values in every interaction with the franchise network. Updates behavioural expectations as the brand evolves and new situations arise.
Franchise Owner / Operator: Ensures the unit culture reflects the values in daily behaviour. Holds the Unit Manager accountable for team culture, not just operational metrics. Participates in values alignment assessments during franchise reviews.
Unit Manager: The critical link between stated values and lived culture. Responsible for coaching, recognising, and correcting behaviours against the standards in this chapter. Leads by example — team members model what they see from their manager, not what they read in a manual.
Team Members: Apply the behavioural standards on every shift. Understand that these are not aspirational suggestions — they are the minimum standard for representing the Creamy brand. Raise any situation where they feel the values are being compromised (see Chapter 3.3 — Grievance procedure).

Measure, Analyse, Improve
Values-in-action are measured through observation, feedback, and culture indicators — not through a checklist alone:
(a) Operational audit — values component: During operational audits, the auditor should assess observable behaviours against this chapter. Are team members remaking substandard products? Are portions consistent with specifications? Is the team engaged and welcoming? Is the environment creating the right atmosphere? This is a qualitative assessment supported by specific observations, not a tick-box exercise.
(b) Customer feedback — values language: Analyse Google reviews and any other customer feedback for language that maps to the four values. Mentions of quality, portion size, warmth, atmosphere, and feeling welcome indicate the values are being lived. Mentions of small portions, indifferent service, or a sterile atmosphere indicate they are not.
(c) Mistake reporting culture: Track whether team members are self-reporting mistakes. A unit where mistakes are surfaced voluntarily has a healthy "Never Give Up" culture. A unit where mistakes are only discovered through investigation or customer complaints does not — and the fix is usually in how the manager responds to admissions, not in the team's character.
(d) Staff retention and morale: High turnover or low morale are leading indicators that values are not being lived at the management level. Exit interview data (where available) should be reviewed for patterns that relate to values failures — particularly around recognition, blame culture, and working atmosphere.
(e) Remake tracking: Track the number of remakes per week. A unit with zero remakes is almost certainly not applying the remake rule — it does not mean every product is perfect, it means team members are not empowered or willing to flag substandard output. A reasonable remake rate indicates the system is working.

Network rollout & change adoption process   [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW]
Introduction
This chapter defines how new or updated procedures are rolled out across the Creamy network and how adoption is verified. Version-controlling the manual (see Update/version control process) ensures the document is accurate; this chapter ensures the people actually change what they do. The distinction matters: a franchisee can acknowledge receipt of an update and still not implement it. Minimum Operating Standards
Every procedural change issued by Head Office must follow a four-step rollout cycle: (1) Communicate — notify all affected franchisees and unit managers of the change, its rationale, and its effective date, with a minimum lead time of. 2 weeks for standard changes, 48 hours for urgent/regulatory changes Right- Done. (2) Train — provide the training or briefing materials needed to implement the change (written guidance, video walkthrough, or in-person briefing depending on complexity). (3) Implement — the change takes effect on the stated date; franchisees confirm implementation. (4) Verify — Head Office confirms adoption through the next scheduled operational audit, a targeted spot-check, or a remote compliance check.
Changes must be classified by impact level: Minor (wording update, no behavioural change required — communicate only), Moderate (new procedure or amended standard — communicate + train), Major (significant operational shift, new system, or regulatory-driven change — full four-step cycle with dedicated training and verification).
Franchisees must confirm implementation of Moderate and Major changes in writing within 5 working days Right- Done of the effective date. Non-confirmation triggers a follow-up from Head Office.
Resistance or non-adoption identified during verification must be escalated to the Franchisee Support function (see Chapter 6.5 — Support model & SLAs). Persistent non-adoption is a performance issue and should be addressed through the remediation process.
A rollout log must be maintained by Head Office recording: change reference, classification, communication date, training delivered, effective date, confirmation received (by unit), and verification outcome.
For multi-unit rollouts, Head Office may designate a pilot unit to test the change before full network deployment. Pilot feedback must be incorporated before the wider rollout proceeds.

Non-negotiables at a glance   [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW - COMPLETED FROM NOTES]
Introduction
This chapter defines the operating standards that are absolute — standards where there is no flexibility, no local interpretation, and no acceptable reason for non-compliance. Every chapter in this manual contains standards, but the items listed here sit above the rest: a breach of any non-negotiable is treated as a serious compliance failure requiring immediate corrective action.
The purpose of separating non-negotiables from general operating standards is clarity. A franchisee managing a busy unit makes dozens of decisions daily, and most of those decisions involve trade-offs. The non-negotiables remove certain decisions from the trade-off equation entirely. These are the lines that cannot be crossed — full stop.
Non-negotiable standards are auditable, and compliance is assessed during every operational visit and formal audit. Repeated or serious breaches may constitute grounds for formal action under the franchise agreement. (See Chapter 6.5 — Underperformance indicators and Chapter 6.6 — Remediation process.)

Minimum Operating Standards
The following standards are non-negotiable across every Creamy location. They are grouped by operational area but carry equal weight — there is no hierarchy within the non-negotiables.
1. Product integrity
(a) Approved products and ingredients only. No franchisee may use a waffle mix, crepe mix, or any other menu component that is not a Creamy-approved product. Every item served must be a confirmed menu item approved by Creamy Head Office. Introducing unapproved products — even as a "test" or "local special" — is a non-negotiable breach.
(b) No ingredient substitution below Creamy standard. Franchisees must not substitute any ingredient for a cheaper or lower-quality alternative than what Creamy Head Office specifies. If a specified supplier is temporarily unavailable, the franchisee must contact Head Office for an approved alternative — not source one independently.
(c) Recipe adherence. All products must be prepared following Creamy's approved recipes exactly. Recipes define method, quantities, and sequence. Deviating from a recipe — whether by changing proportions, skipping steps, or improvising technique — is not permitted.
(d) Serve standard. No product may be served to a customer unless it meets Creamy's standard across all four dimensions: taste, temperature, portion size, and presentation. If a product fails on any one of these, it must be remade — not served. (See Chapter 1.5 — Values in Action: the remake rule.)
2. Food safety and compliance
(e) Mattilsynet inspection result: smiley face required. Every Creamy unit must achieve a smiley face (smile) result on every Mattilsynet food safety inspection. This is the minimum acceptable outcome — not a target, a requirement. If a unit receives anything other than a smiley face, the franchisee must immediately provide Head Office with a written explanation of why, what went wrong, and what corrective actions have been taken. A non-smile result triggers a formal review. (See Chapter 3.6 — HACCP/food safety procedures [NEEDS ADNAN INPUT].)
(f) Labelling and date control. Every product in the kitchen must be labelled and within its expiry date. No exceptions. Unlabelled products or products past their expiry date must be disposed of immediately — they must not be used, repackaged, or relabelled.
(g) Daily checklists — completed every day. The opening checklist, food safety checklist, cleaning checklist, and closing checklist must be completed in full every operating day. Checklists are not optional documentation — they are the evidence that critical safety and operational processes have been carried out. Incomplete or missing checklists are treated as a non-negotiable breach, regardless of whether the underlying tasks were actually performed. (See Chapter 5.1 — Daily operating checklist [NEEDS ADNAN INPUT].)
3. Employment and legal compliance
(h) Employment documentation. Every person working at the unit must be logged into the scheduling/time system (Tidsbanken) and must have a valid, signed employment contract in place. No one works without a contract. No one works off-system. This is both a Creamy requirement and a legal obligation under arbeidsmiljøloven (the Working Environment Act). Non-compliance exposes the franchisee to regulatory penalties and Creamy to reputational risk.
4. Opening hours and punctuality
(i) Opening hours are fixed. The unit must open and close at the times confirmed with Creamy Head Office. Being late to open is a non-negotiable breach — a customer arriving at a unit that should be open and finding it closed is a brand failure and a direct revenue loss. If extraordinary circumstances prevent opening on time (equipment failure, severe weather, emergency), the franchisee must notify Head Office immediately and communicate the closure to customers via the unit's digital channels.
5. Brand and premises standards
(j) No unauthorised changes to the unit. Franchisees must not change the interior colours, signage, décor, or layout of the unit without written approval from Creamy Head Office. The Creamy brand identity is defined centrally (see Chapter 4.1 — Brand guidelines [NEEDS ADNAN INPUT]), and the physical environment of every unit must be consistent with it. Repainting a wall, replacing signage, or rearranging the customer area without approval is a non-negotiable breach.
(k) Uniform compliance. All team members on shift must wear the approved Creamy uniform. The uniform must not be altered, customised, or substituted. If uniform items are damaged or worn, they must be replaced with approved items — not with the team member's own clothing.
6. Equipment readiness
(l) Equipment must be operational. All kitchen and service equipment must be in working order and ready for use before the unit opens and throughout service. Equipment that is broken, underperforming, or unreliable must be repaired or replaced promptly. A franchisee who operates with malfunctioning equipment and allows it to affect product quality or service speed is in breach of this standard. (See Chapter 5.7 — Equipment failure and contingency [if applicable].)

Takes formal action where breaches are identified and not remediated. 2. New Business Setup & Support

2.1  Site selection criteria

Demographic & footfall requirements   [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW - COMPLETED FROM NOTES]
Introduction
This chapter defines where Creamy should and should not open, and who it should serve. Site selection is the highest-stakes decision in franchise growth — a strong operator in the wrong location will underperform, and a weak location cannot be fixed with better marketing or longer hours. Getting the site right is the single most important factor in whether a new Creamy unit succeeds or fails.
This chapter combines two closely linked subjects: the physical site criteria (what kind of location, what kind of street, what facilities) and the target demographic (who lives, works, and socialises in the catchment area). They are inseparable — a location that meets every physical criterion but sits in an area with no target customers is not a viable site.
The site selection criteria documented here are derived from the founder's direct operational experience across three company-owned locations, including the failed shopping centre unit in 2018 (see Chapter 1.1 — Origin Story & Founder Journey). They are not theoretical — they are lessons paid for with real revenue and real losses.

Minimum Operating Standards
PART 1 — SITE CRITERIA
Location type: high street to mid-high street.
Creamy units must be located on high street or mid-high street positions. The distinction is commercial: in some Norwegian cities, the prime high street locations carry rents that do not work against the unit economics of a dessert bar. A mid-high street position — one step back from the absolute prime pitch, but still on a well-trafficked, commercially active street — is an acceptable and often preferable alternative. The key test is footfall and visibility, not prestige of address.
Shopping centre exclusion.
Creamy does not operate in shopping centres. This is a firm rule based on direct experience — a shopping centre unit was opened in 2018, operated at a loss, and was sold within approximately one year (see Chapter 1.1). The reasons are structural, not circumstantial: shopping centre locations impose operating hours the franchisee cannot control, charge turnover-linked rents that erode margin, and attract a transactional footfall pattern that does not align with Creamy's "destination" positioning.
One exception applies: a shopping centre unit may be considered if — and only if — the unit has its own independent street entrance allowing Creamy to set its own opening hours, independent of the centre's operating schedule. This must be confirmed in the lease terms before any further site evaluation proceeds. A unit accessible only through the centre's internal corridors does not qualify, regardless of other merits.
Frontage and visibility.
The unit must have prominent window frontage — a minimum of two large windows is recommended. Creamy's brand relies on visual impact and the ability to see the product and atmosphere from the street. A unit that is hidden, below street level, or set back from the pavement with no window presence will underperform regardless of other criteria.
Catchment area characteristics.
The unit should not be in a purely local or residential neighbourhood. The ideal location is one that connects a broader area — a street or district that draws people from multiple surrounding neighbourhoods and areas, not just the immediate locality. This maximises the size of the addressable customer base and reduces dependence on a single residential catchment.
The site should be located in proximity to:
Shopping centres or major shopping streets (even though Creamy does not operate inside shopping centres, being near one captures the footfall they generate)
Cinemas, bowling centres, or other entertainment and leisure facilities
Other restaurants and food outlets (restaurant clusters generate mutual footfall — customers eat at one place, then walk to another for dessert)
Transport and access.
The location must be easily accessible by public transport — train and bus connections within comfortable walking distance. Parking should be available nearby (on-street, public car park, or shared parking facilities). A location that is difficult to reach by any means other than car, or that has no parking at all, will underperform in the Norwegian market where both public transport and driving are common depending on city and season.
Evening and weekend culture — critical.
The surrounding area must have a strong evening and weekend trading culture. Creamy's peak trading hours are evenings and weekends — when people go out to socialise, treat themselves, or spend time with family and friends. An area that is dominated by offices and empties out after 17:00 on weekdays and at weekends is not viable for Creamy, regardless of daytime footfall. This is a disqualifying criterion, not a preference.
When evaluating a potential site, visit the area on a Friday evening, a Saturday afternoon, and a Sunday. If the streets are quiet and the surrounding businesses are closed, the site fails this test.

PART 2 — TARGET DEMOGRAPHICS
Primary age group: approximately 15–35 years.
Creamy's core customer base falls within the 15–35 age range. This includes late-secondary students, university students, young professionals, and young families. The product, pricing, environment, and brand tone should resonate with this demographic — modern, social, visually appealing, shareable on social media.
This does not mean customers outside this range are unwelcome — they are not. But site selection, marketing, and product development are optimised for this primary group.
Target customer segments:
Families with children. Parents bringing children for a treat or a family outing. The unit environment should be welcoming to families — not a children's play centre, but a place where a family with young children feels comfortable. Menu items and portion options should cater to younger customers alongside the core range.
Couples and friend groups. Social visits — people meeting for dessert as a planned outing or as the second stop after dinner, cinema, or shopping. This is the segment most aligned with Creamy's "happy place" positioning (see Chapter 1.4 — Core Values Statement). The environment must support groups lingering, talking, and enjoying the experience — not a grab-and-go layout.
Students. University and college students represent a significant portion of the 15–35 core demographic, particularly in cities with large student populations (Bergen, Trondheim). Students are price-aware but experience-driven — they choose where to spend carefully but will return regularly to a place they enjoy. Proximity to student areas and university districts is a positive site indicator.
Catchment population profile.
The ideal catchment area has a multicultural local population alongside the broader Norwegian demographic. Creamy's product range and brand positioning appeal across cultural backgrounds, and areas with diverse communities often have stronger evening and weekend food cultures. Site evaluation should consider the demographic composition of the catchment area — not to exclude any group, but to confirm that the mix of people living in and visiting the area aligns with Creamy's broad customer base.
Who Creamy is NOT targeting (for site selection purposes).
Office workers seeking a weekday lunch option — Creamy is not a lunch café
Purely tourist-dependent locations with no local residential or evening customer base (seasonal tourism can supplement, but should not be the primary revenue source)
Very high-income areas where the customer base skews significantly older than 35 and the leisure culture is oriented toward fine dining rather than casual social eating

Introduction
This chapter documents the site types that are excluded from Creamy's franchise model, the commercial and operational reasoning behind each exclusion, and the specific conditions under which a limited exception may be considered. It should be read alongside Chapter 2.2 (Site Selection & Target Demographics), which defines the positive criteria for site selection.
Exclusions exist because certain location types have been tested or assessed and found to be structurally incompatible with Creamy's operating model, brand positioning, or unit economics. These are not preferences — they are conclusions drawn from direct operational experience and from analysis of how Creamy generates revenue.
A prospective franchisee who proposes an excluded site type is not being rejected — their ambition is welcome, but the site is not. This chapter explains why, so that the conversation can move constructively toward identifying a site that works.
