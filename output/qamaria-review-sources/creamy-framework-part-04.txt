Supplier risk & contingency   [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW - COMPLETED FROM NOTES]
Introduction
This chapter addresses what happens when a supplier cannot fulfil an order — a delivery fails, a product is temporarily unavailable, or a supplier relationship ends. It is a short chapter because the honest position is that significant supply disruption is rare in the Norwegian market, and the correct response in almost every scenario is the same: contact Head Office.
Norway's food wholesale market is concentrated. For most product categories, there are not multiple equivalent suppliers offering the same product at the same standard. This is precisely why Creamy operates with a single approved supplier per category (see Chapter 2.7 — Approved Supplier List) — it guarantees consistent quality, consistent shelf life, and a traceable supply chain. The trade-off is that switching suppliers is not a quick fix when something goes wrong, and franchisees should not attempt it independently.
This chapter exists to ensure that franchisees know what to do — and, equally importantly, what not to do — if a supply issue arises.

Minimum Operating Standards
The core principle: Head Office resolves supply disruptions. Franchisees do not.
If an approved supplier cannot provide a product, the franchisee must contact Head Office. Head Office will either source an approved alternative from the same supplier (most suppliers carry similar or substitute products), identify a temporary approved replacement from another source, or confirm that the product should be removed from the menu temporarily until supply is restored.
The franchisee must not independently source a replacement product from a non-approved supplier, purchase from a retail shop, or substitute with a different product from the approved supplier's range without Head Office confirmation. This is not bureaucracy — it is the only way to guarantee that what ends up in the customer's dessert meets Creamy's quality, allergen, and traceability standards.
Scenario-by-scenario guidance:
Scenario 1 — Short-term product unavailability (supplier is temporarily out of stock).
This is the most common situation and is usually resolved within one or two delivery cycles.
Action: Contact Head Office. In most cases, the supplier will have a similar or equivalent product available. Head Office will confirm whether the alternative is approved for use. If no suitable alternative is available from the supplier, Head Office will advise whether to temporarily remove the affected menu item or source from an alternative approved channel.
Do not: Assume that a "similar-looking" product from the same supplier is an acceptable substitute without Head Office confirmation. Product specifications (ingredients, allergens, portion sizes, flavour profiles) may differ.
Scenario 2 — Delivery failure (order placed but not received or received incomplete).
Action: Contact the supplier directly to resolve the delivery issue (reschedule, partial redelivery). If the issue cannot be resolved in time for service, contact Head Office for guidance. If a critical product is missing and the unit cannot operate its core menu, Head Office will advise on the best course of action — which may include temporary menu adjustment or emergency sourcing.
Do not: Go to a retail shop or local wholesale to "fill the gap." The product will not be the same specification, it will not carry the correct traceability, and it may not meet Creamy's quality standard.
Scenario 3 — Supplier discontinues a product permanently.
This is rare but does happen — a supplier may discontinue a product line, change a recipe, or exit a product category.
Action: Head Office will have a replacement ready. When a supplier signals that a product is being discontinued, Head Office will identify, test, and approve a replacement before the current supply runs out. Franchisees will be notified of the change with sufficient lead time to adjust their ordering.
Do not: Attempt to stockpile the outgoing product beyond normal stock levels (this creates waste and storage problems), or source the "same" product from another supplier without Head Office approval (it may be a different formulation, different allergen profile, or different quality).
Scenario 4 — Supplier relationship ends (Creamy changes supplier for a category).
Head Office may change an approved supplier — for example, if a better option becomes available, if the current supplier's quality deteriorates, or if pricing terms change unfavourably.
Action: Head Office will manage the transition. Franchisees will receive the new supplier details, account setup instructions, updated product codes, and a transition timeline. Franchisees must complete the transition within the specified timeframe.
Do not: Continue ordering from the outgoing supplier after the transition deadline. Do not negotiate independently with the outgoing supplier to retain a relationship.
Scenario 5 — Local fruit supplier issue (where Egil Jensen does not deliver).
Franchisees operating with an approved local fruit supplier (see Chapter 2.7) are responsible for managing that relationship day to day. If the local supplier cannot deliver:
Action: Contact Head Office. Head Office will advise on whether an alternative local supplier can be approved, whether fruit products should be temporarily removed from the menu, or whether ASKO (the main supplier) can provide acceptable fruit products as a short-term substitute.
Do not: Source fruit from a retail shop or unapproved supplier without Head Office confirmation.

Quality/spec standards for ingredients   [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW - COMPLETED FROM NOTES]
Introduction
This chapter explains why Creamy's ingredient specifications exist — not just what to buy (that is covered in Chapter 2.7 — Approved Supplier List) but why specific quality grades matter and what goes wrong when they are substituted. It is the chapter a franchisee should read when they are tempted to save money by switching to a cheaper version of something.
Every ingredient specification in Creamy's product range exists because it has been tested. In many cases, the cheaper alternative has also been tested — and rejected. The founder and Head Office have already made the mistakes that a cost-cutting franchisee would make, and this chapter documents the lessons so they do not need to be repeated at the customer's expense.
The underlying principle is simple: Creamy's product quality is built on the interaction between specific ingredients, specific recipes, and specific techniques. Change one ingredient and the product changes — sometimes obviously, sometimes subtly, but always in a way the customer will eventually notice. "Quality Always First" (see Chapter 1.4 — Core Values) is not an abstract value when it comes to ingredients — it is a purchasing discipline.

Minimum Operating Standards
THE PRINCIPLE: SPECIFICATION EXISTS FOR A REASON
Every ingredient used in Creamy products is specified by Head Office — the supplier, the brand, the grade, and in some cases the exact product code. These specifications are not arbitrary preferences. Each one reflects a tested relationship between the ingredient and the final product outcome. The sections below illustrate this with specific examples from Creamy's core product range.
Franchisees must use the specified ingredients exactly as listed in the approved product list (see Chapter 2.7). The examples below explain why — so that the reasoning is understood, not just the rule.

CHOCOLATE — COCOA PERCENTAGE MATTERS
Creamy uses chocolate at a specific cocoa percentage. This is not a premium label or a marketing choice — it is a flavour and performance specification. Chocolate with a different cocoa percentage will taste different in the finished dessert, even if the difference seems small on the packaging.
A lower cocoa percentage produces a sweeter, less complex flavour and a different melt profile. Sauces and toppings will be thinner and less rich.
A higher cocoa percentage produces a more bitter, more intense flavour that may overpower other dessert components.
The specified percentage is calibrated to Creamy's recipes. The recipes assume a specific sweetness, viscosity, and flavour intensity from the chocolate. Change the chocolate and the recipe no longer produces the intended result.
The chocolate specification is supplied by Creamy Drift AS (see Chapter 2.7). There is no acceptable substitute from another supplier.

BUTTER — AFFECTS WAFFLE TEXTURE AND APPEARANCE
The waffle mix from Vaffelprinsen AS is designed to work with a specific type of butter. Using a different butter — particularly a cheaper alternative with a different fat content, water content, or composition — produces a measurably different result:
Crispness: The right butter gives the golden waffle its characteristic crisp exterior. A cheaper butter with higher water content produces a softer, less satisfying texture that does not hold through service.
Colour: Butter quality directly affects the golden colour of the finished waffle. A cheaper butter may produce a paler or less even result.
Flavour: Butter is a primary flavour carrier in the waffle. A lower-quality butter tastes different, and the customer will notice — particularly repeat customers who know what a Creamy waffle should taste like.
The specified butter is ordered through ASKO. The product code is provided in the Creamy ordering list. Substitution is not permitted.

CREAM — CONSISTENCY AND TASTE
Cheaper cream products often have a lower fat content, contain stabilisers or thickeners, or are partially reconstituted. These differences affect the finished dessert in two ways:
Consistency: Lower-fat or stabiliser-heavy cream does not whip, set, or pour the same way. Desserts that rely on cream for body and texture will be visibly and texturally different — less rich, less stable, more likely to separate or weep during service.
Taste: Cream is not a neutral carrier — it has its own flavour profile. Cheaper cream tastes different. In a dessert where cream is a major component, the difference is obvious.
The specified cream is ordered through ASKO. There is no "equivalent but cheaper" option that produces the same result.

CHOCOLATE SPREAD — NO SUBSTITUTE MATCHES NUTELLA
Creamy uses Nutella as its chocolate spread. This is a tested decision, not a default. Head Office has trialled multiple cheaper chocolate spread alternatives. None of them match the flavour, texture, or customer expectation.
Customers know what Nutella tastes like. Serving a cheaper alternative and calling it "chocolate spread" may not trigger an immediate complaint, but it changes the product and erodes trust. The cost difference per portion between Nutella and a budget alternative is marginal; the brand damage from a noticeable quality drop is not.
Nutella is ordered through ASKO. Substitution with any other chocolate spread is not permitted.

FRESH FRUIT AND BERRIES — QUALITY DETERMINES COST, NOT THE OTHER WAY AROUND
Cheap fruit is not cheap. This is counterintuitive, but it is the most common ingredient false economy in food service:
Lower-quality fruit and berries have a shorter usable life — often no more than two days before they deteriorate visibly (bruising, softening, discolouration, mould).
Shorter shelf life means more waste. Fruit that is thrown away after two days costs more per usable portion than better-quality fruit that lasts four or five days.
Lower-quality fruit also looks worse on the plate. Dessert presentation is a brand standard (see Chapter 2.3 — Non-Negotiable Operating Standards). Bruised strawberries or soft blueberries on a plated dessert undermine the product visually before the customer takes a bite.
The calculation is straightforward: buy quality fruit from the approved supplier (Egil Jensen, or an approved local alternative — see Chapter 2.7), manage your stock rotation properly (first in, first out), and you will waste less and present better. Buy cheap fruit and you will throw more away, serve a worse-looking product, and spend more in total.

GENERAL QUALITY SPECIFICATION RULES
(a) No downgrade substitution. Franchisees must not substitute any specified ingredient with a cheaper or lower-grade alternative. This applies even if the packaging looks similar, the product name is similar, or the supplier's salesperson describes it as "equivalent."
(b) If in doubt, ask. If a franchisee is unsure whether a product matches the specification — for example, if the supplier has updated its packaging and the product code has changed — they must contact Head Office before using it. A quick check prevents a quality failure.
(c) Taste-test discipline. If a product tastes, looks, or performs differently from normal — even if it is from the approved supplier with the correct product code — stop using it and report to Head Office. Suppliers occasionally change formulations without notice. Head Office needs to know immediately so the issue can be investigated and, if necessary, the specification updated or the supplier challenged.
(d) Cost is managed through waste reduction, not ingredient quality. If a franchisee's food cost is above target, the solution is never to buy cheaper ingredients. The solution is in waste control, stock management (see Chapter 5.3), portion consistency (not reduction — see Chapter 1.4), and pricing (see Chapter 6.2 — Pricing review framework). Ingredient quality is fixed; everything else is variable.

Roles & Responsibilities
Creamy Head Office / Creamy Drift AS: Defines and maintains all ingredient specifications. Tests alternatives before approving any specification change. Communicates specification changes to franchisees with clear reasoning and transition timelines. Investigates reports of ingredient quality variation from franchisees or from its own quality checks.
Franchise Owner / Operator: Purchases only specified ingredients from approved suppliers. Does not substitute, downgrade, or independently trial alternatives. Manages food cost through operational efficiency, not ingredient compromise. Reports any ingredient quality concern to Head Office.
Unit Manager: Checks deliveries against specifications on receipt — correct product, correct brand, correct quality. Stores ingredients correctly to maximise usable life (particularly fresh fruit, dairy, and chocolate). Manages stock rotation (first in, first out). Reports any ingredient that looks, tastes, or performs differently to the franchisee and Head Office.
Team Members: Follow recipes using the specified ingredients. Report to the Unit Manager if any ingredient seems different from normal — different taste, different colour, different texture, different packaging. Do not use a product you are unsure about without checking.

Measure, Analyse, Improve
(a) Ingredient compliance audit. During operational audits, verify that all ingredients in use match the approved specification. Check product labels, brands, and supplier origin. Any non-specified ingredient is a non-negotiable breach (see Chapter 2.3).
(b) Waste tracking by ingredient category. Monitor waste rates for high-value perishable ingredients (fresh fruit, dairy, cream). High waste rates may indicate poor stock rotation, over-ordering, or inadequate storage — all of which are fixable without touching ingredient quality. Conversely, very low waste rates combined with customer complaints about freshness or appearance may indicate that products are being used beyond their optimal quality window.
(c) Product consistency monitoring. Periodically compare the finished product across units. If Unit A's golden waffle looks or tastes different from Unit B's, the cause is almost always an ingredient variation, a recipe deviation, or an equipment issue. Investigate systematically rather than assuming "every kitchen is different."
(d) Supplier quality tracking. Log any instance where an approved ingredient arrives below expected quality (damaged, short-dated, visually substandard). Report centrally so Head Office can address patterns with the supplier.

Introduction
This chapter defines the standard launch plan for every new Creamy unit. A launch is not just an opening day — it is a planned campaign that builds awareness before the doors open, creates an event that draws a crowd on day one, and sustains momentum through the critical first week of trading.
The temptation for a new franchisee is to be conservative with launch spending — they have just invested in fit-out, equipment, stock, and staff, and the instinct is to protect cash. This is the wrong instinct at the wrong moment. A strong launch generates immediate revenue, creates word-of-mouth, builds the social media content library, and establishes the unit as a local destination from day one. The cost of a strong launch is recovered quickly; the cost of a quiet opening lingers for months.
The founder's approach has always been to make the opening a big event — give away product, offer meaningful discounts, create a buzz. This chapter codifies that approach into a repeatable framework for every new unit.

Minimum Operating Standards
PHASE 1 — PRE-LAUNCH (3 WEEKS BEFORE OPENING)
