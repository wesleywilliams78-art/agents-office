Cash flow forecasting & monitoring   [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW]
Introduction
This chapter defines the standard process for forecasting, monitoring, and managing cash flow at unit and entity level. A profitable unit can still fail if it runs out of cash — profit is an accounting concept; cash is what pays the bills. This is the foundation that Chapter 6.4 (Readiness criteria for site #2) assumes exists: the requirement that expansion capital be secured 'without jeopardising Site #1's cash position' is meaningless without a disciplined cash flow forecast and monitoring cadence.
Minimum Operating Standards
A rolling 13-week cash flow forecast must be maintained by the Franchise Owner (or their finance lead) and updated weekly. The 13-week horizon covers one full quarter and is the standard minimum for operational cash management in SMEs.
The forecast must be structured as a weekly cash-in / cash-out matrix covering at minimum: (a) Cash in — daily/weekly sales receipts (from EPOS), other income (e.g. catering, events). (b) Cash out — supplier payments (by supplier, by due date), payroll and employer costs (including Arbeidsgiveravgift — employer's social security contribution), rent, utilities, loan/lease repayments, MVA (VAT) payments, tax instalments (forskuddsskatt), insurance premiums, marketing spend, maintenance/repairs, and any one-off capex. (c) Net cash flow per week and cumulative cash balance.
The forecast must be reconciled against the actual bank balance weekly. Any variance exceeding [Adnan to confirm: e.g. NOK 10,000 or 10%]Right of forecast must be investigated and the forecast adjusted.
A minimum cash reserve — the lowest acceptable bank balance — must be defined and maintained. [Adnan to confirm: target minimum cash reserve — industry norm for a single-unit QSR is typically 4-8 weeks of fixed costs]3 month. If the forecast projects the cash balance falling below the minimum reserve at any point in the 13-week horizon, the Franchise Owner must take corrective action immediately (defer discretionary spend, accelerate receivables, draw on credit facility if available).
Cash flow reporting: a cash flow summary must be included in the monthly business review (see Chapter 6.3 — Monthly business review) covering: opening balance, total receipts, total payments, closing balance, and variance vs. forecast.
Before any capital expenditure commitment (see Chapter 6.6 — Capex authorisation framework), the Franchise Owner must demonstrate via the cash flow forecast that the expenditure can be absorbed without breaching the minimum cash reserve for the following 13 weeks.
Seasonal cash flow planning: once a full year of trading data is available, the forecast must incorporate known seasonal patterns (e.g. lower revenue in January, higher costs in December, annual insurance premium, MVA quarter-end payments). Head Office should provide a seasonal cash flow adjustment template based on network-wide patterns.
Roles & Responsibilities
Franchise Owner / Operator: Owns the cash flow forecast; updates it weekly; takes corrective action when the reserve threshold is threatened; presents cash flow position at the monthly business review.
Unit Manager: Provides the revenue and operational cost data that feeds the forecast (daily sales, supplier invoices received, maintenance costs incurred).
Head Office: Provides the forecasting template; reviews cash flow position as part of the monthly business review; flags network-wide cash flow risks (e.g. supplier price increases, regulatory cost changes).
External Accountant (if retained): Assists with tax instalment calculations, MVA timing, and annual cash flow planning.
Measure Analyse Improve
Track: (a) forecast accuracy — actual vs. predicted cash balance, weekly and monthly, (b) number of weeks the cash balance fell below the minimum reserve, (c) average days' cash on hand (closing balance ÷ average daily operating cost), (d) variance between forecast and actual on each major cost line. A forecast that is consistently inaccurate in one direction (e.g. always overestimates receipts) indicates a systematic bias that must be corrected.

Working capital cycle management   [YSF ADVISORY — DRAFTED, PENDING REVIEW]
Introduction
This chapter defines how Creamy manages its working capital cycle — the time and cash tied up between paying suppliers and receiving payment from customers. In a QSR operation where most sales are cash or immediate card settlement, the working capital cycle is typically short and favourable: customers pay instantly, but suppliers grant credit terms. The risk is not the cycle itself — it is losing control of it: paying suppliers early, holding too much stock, or failing to enforce agreed credit terms. This chapter provides the framework for keeping cash circulating efficiently.
Minimum Operating Standards
The working capital cycle for each unit must be understood and documented: (a) Inventory holding period — average days from receiving stock to selling it (target: [Adnan to confirm: e.g. 3-7 days for perishables, 14-30 days for dry goods and packaging])Right. (b) Receivables period — for a QSR with predominantly cash/card sales, this is effectively zero (same-day settlement from card processor, minus any processing delay). [Adnan to confirm: card processor settlement terms — typically T+1 or T+2 in Norway]3 days. (c) Payables period — average days from receiving a supplier invoice to paying it (target: use the full agreed credit terms, typically [Adnan to confirm: e.g. 14 or 30 days net])Asko 10 days, Tingstad 30 days, coca cola 14 days, solberg og hansen 30 days.
Payment discipline: supplier invoices must be paid on the due date — not before (which gives away free credit) and not after (which damages supplier relationships and may incur late-payment fees or loss of credit terms). A payment run schedule must be established (e.g. weekly on a fixed day) and adhered to.
Stock levels must be managed to minimise cash tied up in inventory without risking stock-outs. Par levels (see Chapter 5.3 — Ordering cycle & par levels) should be calibrated to the demand forecast (see Chapter 3.4 — Demand forecasting). Over-ordering ties up cash; under-ordering loses sales.
Card processor terms: verify and document the settlement terms with the card payment provider. If the processor holds funds for more than T+2, evaluate whether a better settlement arrangement is available. Even a one-day improvement in settlement terms can improve cash flow materially over a year.
MVA (VAT) management: Norwegian food service businesses charge different MVA rates (25% for dine-in, 15% for takeaway). The MVA collected belongs to the state and must be remitted per the termin (reporting period — typically bi-monthly for small businesses). It must not be treated as available cash. Best practice: transfer the estimated MVA liability to a separate holding account after each MVA period to avoid a cash crunch at the payment deadline.
Working capital monitoring: the Franchise Owner must review the working capital position monthly as part of the monthly business review, covering: current stock value, outstanding supplier payables (total and ageing), card processor receivables in transit, and MVA liability accrued.
